Monday morning: you open one competitor product page, then another. A price seems lower. But when did it change? Which variant is on sale? Is delivery still extra? Ecommerce competitor monitoring should help you answer these questions without turning every working day into a tour of online shops.

The starting point is not to track everything. Decide which business decisions you need to inform, which pages contain the evidence and how your team will handle a change. This guide sets out a practical approach, from your first spreadsheet to automated alerts.

THE SHORT ANSWER

Ecommerce competitor monitoring means regularly checking relevant competitor offers and using the changes to inform your decisions. Choose a defined set of pages, compare equivalent offers and check the context before acting. Automation handles repeated checks; it does not replace your judgement.

01. What decisions should competitor monitoring support?

Competitive monitoring means observing developments in your market to inform your choices. For an ecommerce manager, it can support a pricing review, a planned promotion or an assessment of a changing product range. Each goal calls for different sources.

Ask your team a simple question: “If this information changes, what will we do?” A price change on a key product could prompt a margin check. A new seasonal collection might belong on the agenda for your next range review. Information with no clear use can simply add noise.

The France Num resources on monitoring and market research (in French) place this work in a broader business development context. Competitor page monitoring is one part of that work, alongside your customer knowledge and internal data.

02. Which competitor pages should you monitor?

Start with a few comparable competitors: similar customers, relevant categories and a delivery area that overlaps yours. Separate direct competitors from retailers you use as inspiration. A general retailer might offer merchandising ideas without being the right price benchmark for every item.

Then choose specific URLs. A product page helps you track an individual item; a collection page may show part of a range; a delivery page explains commercial terms. Recording only the website name is not enough: you need to know which page provides which signal.

Match each monitored page to a business question

For each URL, record the person responsible, the information you want and the last check. Replace a page when it no longer answers your question. A well-chosen scope matters more than a long list.

03. How do you compare competitor prices fairly?

A displayed price drop may matter without justifying a matching discount. Check the item, variant, quantity, currency and purchase conditions before interpreting it. A members-only price or a discount code is not directly comparable with your standard public price.

Fictional example: a bag drops from €99 to €79 on a monitored page. That is €20 less, or approximately 20.2% of the original price. But a clearance offer on one colour calls for a different interpretation from a permanent reduction across the range.

The Google Merchant Center product data specification distinguishes variants, prices and availability. It describes feeds supplied to Google, but also offers a useful reference for the attributes to include in your own comparison checklist.

Likewise, an item disappearing briefly does not prove it is permanently out of stock. A different sort order, selected variant or temporarily inaccessible page could explain what you see. Keep the source and observation date so you can return to the original evidence.

04. Manual checks or automated competitor monitoring?

A shared spreadsheet is enough to start: URL, product or offer, observed value, date, comment and possible action. Manual checks help you understand each page and its quirks. They remain useful for occasional research or a closer reading of a new commercial offer.

The challenge is repetition. You need to reopen the same pages, compare them with earlier observations and keep consistent notes. As your scope grows, that routine can become irregular or depend on one person.

Automation helps with repeated checks on technically compatible pages. It does not remove the need to choose suitable URLs or interpret alerts. A straightforward approach is to automate checks on key pages and reserve time for reviewing changes and making decisions.

06. What are the limits of automated page monitoring?

One URL does not mean an entire catalogue. On a paginated collection, products on later pages may not be extracted. Progressive loading, sorting and page structure can also affect the accessible portion. Ask exactly which data is monitored before drawing conclusions about the whole website.

Pages requiring a login are not supported. JavaScript, protections against automated access and structural changes can limit or prevent extraction from public pages. Compatibility checked during setup is not a promise that every website will always work.

Frequency matters too. A promotion can appear and disappear between checks. ChangeWatch does not provide real-time monitoring or guarantee detection of every change. Set expectations around your actual use case rather than exhaustive coverage.

Finally, publicly accessible content is not automatically free of all restrictions. If your project involves personal data, consult the CNIL recommendations on reusing data published online (in French). For commercial monitoring, focus on the product information and offer conditions needed for your objective.

07. What should you do after a competitor alert?

Assign someone to review alerts. Without a clear owner, a notification can fall between teams. The aim is not to react to everything, but to distinguish a change worth examining from one with no consequence for your business.

  1. Confirm the facts. Open the source and check the variant, date and visible conditions.
  2. Assess the impact. Is this a key product? Does your margin allow a response? Is the offer genuinely comparable?
  3. Choose a next step. Adjust merchandising, investigate further, plan a promotion or make no change.
  4. Keep a record. Note the signal, reasoning and decision for your next team review.

A new free-delivery threshold, for example, might prompt you to clarify how you present shipping costs rather than reduce prices. A new collection could inform a range review without demanding immediate action. Monitoring helps you choose your response, not copy every competitor move.
