Competitor price monitoring: a practical operating procedure

A repeatable process for checking offers, handling uncertainty and handing over reliable evidence.

Competitor price monitoring means checking competing offers repeatedly so you can identify a change worth verifying. To make it useful, define the exact product and page, choose a check schedule, separate valid observations from failed readings, and assign someone to review alerts. A detected change is an input to a decision, not an instruction to cut your price.

This guide is a working procedure for an e-commerce team monitoring a shortlist of offers. It focuses on what happens between a scheduled check and a verified observation. For software selection, use our price tracking software buying guide; for calculating gaps and making a pricing case, use our competitor price analysis method.

Three separate jobs: monitoring observes changes over time; analysis interprets them using context and your own business data; automatic repricing changes your selling prices under configured rules. ChangeWatch supports selected-page monitoring. It does not perform automatic repricing or strategic analysis.

Define an offer you can recognise at the next check

Start with a stable public product URL and write down which offer it represents. Product matching means deciding whether two listings describe the same item or a genuinely comparable alternative. A similar name or photograph is not enough: capacity, pack size, model generation, condition and seller can change the comparison.

A GTIN is a trade-item identifier used to identify a product. Google’s GTIN documentation describes its use in product data. An identifier can help confirm a match, but you should still verify the variant and offer conditions. ChangeWatch does not automatically match a whole catalogue.

Choose a check schedule and a response window

Choose frequency from two questions: how short could a relevant offer be, and when can your team review it? More observations do not help if no one owns the response. Agree on a review window and a backup owner rather than treating every alert as an emergency.

Fictitious timing example: checks at 09:00 on Monday and Tuesday leave a gap of 24 hours. A promotion running from Monday 12:00 to 18:00 could be missed by both checks. If Tuesday’s check detects a change, you know it was observed on Tuesday; you do not know its exact start time from those two observations alone.

For a planned campaign, review whether the configured schedule and your own availability fit the decision you need to make. If you need continuous observation or guaranteed capture of short promotions, daily page checks are not a substitute. ChangeWatch offers daily checks on Starter and Business and up to three daily checks on Pro, not real-time monitoring.

Give every observation a status before acting

A false positive is a signal that looks like the change you wanted to detect but turns out to have another explanation: for example, a different variant became the default. The opposite risk also matters: no alert does not prove that every offer stayed unchanged.

Use the following statuses in your own review process. These are proposed team labels, not named ChangeWatch features. They help prevent a failed collection from silently becoming evidence of a stable price.

Why a readable page can still be an uncertain observation

A dynamic page loads or changes some information after opening, often using JavaScript. The displayed price may depend on a selected variant, region or session. A new page layout may also move the information outside the portion being read. These are reasons to check compatibility, not to assume a blank result means a free product or an out-of-stock item.

If a source requires a login, blocks access or no longer exposes the relevant information, review the source choice. Do not make an unsupported conclusion from a partial reading. With ChangeWatch, compatibility depends on the public page and its accessible content.

Work through three alerts before changing a price

A lower displayed price can require no immediate response

Suppose A’s €89 offer has €8 delivery for the basket and destination being compared. That is €89 + €8 = €97. If B’s €95 offer includes delivery under those same conditions, B is €2 lower on that comparison. This is arithmetic for the fictitious scenario, not a claim that delivery is always captured automatically.

Check any promotion end date and eligibility wording. Google Merchant Center’s price guidance illustrates why product price and offer conditions need consistent treatment. Those are rules for submitting product data to Google, not a guarantee that a competitor’s page is complete or accurate.

An unchanged price still needs offer context

B’s displayed price has not moved, but its availability or delivery threshold might have. Conversely, a product disappearing from a category page does not prove that it is unavailable. Verify the product page and the exact variant. If the information cannot be confirmed, keep the observation uncertain.

A conditional price is not automatically comparable

If C’s €92 requires buying two units, it is not a €92 single-item alternative for a customer who wants one. Record the condition before circulating an alert as a confirmed comparable drop. Then ask whether your own margin, service proposition and customer needs justify a response. Monitoring alone cannot answer that question.

Know when a shortlist becomes a catalogue project

Monitoring a selected product page answers a narrow question about that page. Catalogue-scale monitoring additionally requires discovering products, maintaining matches across sellers, managing variants and keeping track of coverage. Those tasks do not become solved merely because a category URL is being checked.

Pagination splits a listing across multiple pages or loading steps. A product may move to another page because the sorting order changed. A “load more” button can expose items that were not present in the first portion. Record which parts are actually covered and do not treat the first visible set as the whole catalogue.

The distinction also exists in specialist products: Prisync distinguishes URL-based, channel-based and hybrid monitoring. This is a useful illustration of different scopes, not a claim that ChangeWatch offers these modes or a recommendation to buy a particular platform.

If your requirement is a normalised historical price database, bulk product matching or large exports, write those requirements explicitly and choose a system designed for them. Close the monitoring task with a short evidence note

Once a change is verified, send the decision owner enough information to assess it without treating your interpretation as fact. A useful handoff contains:

  1. The source URL and the time of the last successful observation.
  2. The exact variant, market and offer conditions checked.
  3. The previous observation and current observation, clearly separated.
  4. What remains unknown, including delivery, stock or promotion duration.
  5. The person responsible for deciding whether a response is justified and when to review it.

Do not fill an unknown field with a guess. “No pricing change recommended yet; promotion duration unconfirmed” can be a useful result. Repeated alerts for the same unresolved candidate should stay attached to that review rather than create multiple independent decisions.

At a regular team review, remove sources that no longer answer a business question and investigate sources repeatedly left uncertain. This is your operational routine, not an automatic quality report provided by ChangeWatch. Operational questions

Does competitor price monitoring require repricing?

No. You can monitor an offer, confirm a change and deliberately keep your own price. Automatic repricing is a separate capability with separate controls; ChangeWatch does not provide it.

How often should a team check competitor prices?

There is no universal interval. Consider the duration of relevant offers, source compatibility and the time your team can respond. Scheduled checks can miss changes between observations, even when performed several times a day.

Can I treat no alert as proof that prices stayed the same?

No. A change may have occurred between checks or outside the accessible portion. Confirm the source and the time of the last valid observation when the decision requires fresh evidence.
